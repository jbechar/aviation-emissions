How the carbon money works

Airlines pay for carbon in two different ways. Inside Europe, they buy permits from the government. On most other international flights, they buy carbon credits from projects that cut pollution somewhere else. This page starts with credits, then shows where airlines pay on top of them.

I last checked prices on 21 November 2026. Carbon prices change every day, so use them as a rough guide.

It all comes down to one tonne

A carbon market turns climate results into units you can buy, sell and use up. To compare a cookstove in Rwanda with a forest in Guyana, every unit means the same thing: one tonne of CO₂, or the same warming from other gases (written tCO₂e).

A credit and an allowance are both counted in tonnes, but they point in opposite directions. An allowance is a permit to pollute. A credit is proof that pollution was cut. Governments make allowances by passing a law with a cap. Credits are made by projects, under rules written mostly by private groups.

Two floors

Think of it as a building. The ground floor is where units come from. The top floor is where airlines hand them in. The two European schemes stand on a government cap. CORSIA and voluntary claims stand on carbon credits. Pick a scheme to follow its money.

The ground floor: how a credit is made

A credit goes through the same steps whoever ends up buying it. It can take 2 to 5 years from the first idea to the first credits, so projects often need money long before they earn any. Step through it below.

Who does what

Many groups touch one credit. Each one has a single job, and most of them are paid a fee for it.

Where does the money actually go?

It depends on the floor.

EU and UK ETS. The airline buys allowances at a government auction or from another company. Auction money goes to governments. None of it goes to a project. The climate gain comes from the cap: there are fewer allowances each year, so the total pollution has to fall.

CORSIA and voluntary claims. The airline pays a broker or the developer for credits. Most of the money ends up with the project, after the broker's margin and the fees for the standard, the registry and the auditors. This is the money that pays for the stoves, panels or trees. That is why it matters so much that the tonnes are real.

Why CORSIA credits are hard to find

Not every credit counts for CORSIA. It has to come from one of the ten programmes ICAO has approved for 2024 to 2026. The host country also has to send a letter (a "letter of authorization"). In it, the country agrees to take that tonne off its own climate total, so the same tonne is not counted twice. This is called a corresponding adjustment.

Few countries had sent these letters, so supply has been short. Airlines need more than 200 million credits for 2024 to 2026 flights, and they have until 31 January 2028 to hand them in. A missed deadline is a compliance problem, so buying late is a real risk.

Not every credit is as good as its label

Standards are needed. Without them there is no shared idea of what counts. But they are not perfect. Studies and news reports have found some problems:

  • Some recipes let projects claim more tonnes than they really saved, mostly in forest and land projects.
  • Rules were slow to change, even when new evidence said they were too generous.
  • Standards earn fees on each project and credit, which can pull against being strict.
  • Land disputes and unfair sharing of money were not always caught early.

Standards are now tightening their rules. The ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles set a shared bar, and credits that meet it can carry a label. Still, a stamp from a standard does not prove a credit is good. It is worth looking at the project itself.
