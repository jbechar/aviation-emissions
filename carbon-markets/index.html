Where airlines' carbon money goes

Airlines pay for carbon in two ways. In Europe, they buy permits from the government. Under CORSIA, they buy carbon credits from projects that cut pollution somewhere else. Here is which one a flight pays, what it costs, and where the money ends up.

Prices checked 21 November 2026. They change every day.

A permit and a credit are both one tonne. They point opposite ways.

An allowance lets you put a tonne of CO₂ into the air. A credit shows a tonne was cut or taken out somewhere else. The EU and UK schemes only take allowances. CORSIA only takes credits.

Before you buy a CORSIA credit, check five things

Supply is short. Airlines need more than 200 million eligible credits for 2024 to 2026, and a credit that fails one of these does not count.

  1. It comes from an approved programmeICAO has approved ten programmes for 2024 to 2026. A credit from anywhere else does not count, however good it is.
  2. The host country has signed offThe country where the project sits sends a letter of authorization. It agrees to take that tonne off its own climate total, so the tonne is not counted twice.
  3. The project is recent enoughIts first crediting period started on or after 1 January 2016.
  4. The tonnes were cut in 2021 to 2026This is the credit's vintage. Older vintages do not count for this period.
  5. It is labelled and cancelled in timeThe registry marks it as CORSIA-eligible. The airline cancels it by 31 January 2028.

For voluntary claims, nobody checks for you

There is no rulebook, so quality varies a lot. Some methods have let projects claim more tonnes than they saved, mostly in forests. Standards earn a fee on every credit, and they have been slow to tighten up.

The ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles label is the closest thing to a shared bar. Even then, look at the project itself before you put your name to a claim.
